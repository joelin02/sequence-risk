1. What sequence risk is

While you are saving, the order your returns arrive in doesn't matter. A bad decade followed by a good one leaves you in exactly the same place as a good decade followed by a bad one, because multiplication doesn't care about order.

That stops being true the moment you start withdrawing. Every withdrawal made while prices are low sells more of the portfolio to raise the same dollars, and what was sold isn't there to recover when prices come back. Sequence risk (or sequence-of-returns risk) is the danger that the bad years land early in retirement, when the portfolio is at its largest and has the most years of withdrawals still ahead of it.

The illustration below uses made-up numbers to isolate the effect. Two retirees each start with and withdraw a year for years. They earn exactly the same set of yearly returns, with the same average. The only difference is the order: one gets the weak years first, the other gets the same years in reverse.

This is why a single average return is a poor guide to whether a retirement plan works. What matters is what happens in the first ten years or so. The backtester answers the question directly: it takes your plan and starts it in every month of history you give it, so you can see how many starting points would have survived and which ones would not.

2. Preparing your data

You load three CSV files. Each has two columns, a date and a value:

Date,Value
1987-01-31,274.08
1987-02-28,284.20
1987-03-31,291.70
  • Stocks: a stock index level. Use a total-return index (dividends reinvested) if you can; a price-only index understates returns.
  • Bonds: a bond index level, again ideally total-return.
  • Inflation: a price index such as CPI. This is the index level itself, not a yearly percentage rate.

Format rules

  • A header row is optional; it is skipped automatically.
  • Dates like 1987-01-31 are the safest. Other formats your browser can parse usually work too.
  • Values should be plain numbers with no thousands separators (1234.5, not 1,234.5).
  • The backtest runs monthly. If a file has several rows in one calendar month (daily or weekly data), the last one in that month is used.
  • The day of the month doesn't need to match between files. Rows are matched by year and month.
  • There can be no missing months inside the overlapping period. If there is a gap, the app tells you where it is instead of running.

Only the period covered by all three files is used. After loading, the Common backtest range line shows that period. It needs to be longer than the retirement you want to test, and the longer it is, the more starting points you get.

3. Configuring your portfolio

FieldWhat it means
Initial portfolio sizeWhat you retire with, in dollars.
Years in retirementHow long the money has to last. Each tested period is exactly this long.
Annual spendingWhat you withdraw in the first year. One twelfth is taken each month, and the amount rises every month in step with the inflation index, so your buying power stays constant.
Real yield on inflation-adjusted bondsThe yearly return above inflation for the third asset (see below). It can be zero or negative.
Portfolio allocationThe split between stocks, bonds, and inflation-adjusted bonds. It must total 100%.
RebalancingHow often the portfolio is reset to that split. With "No Rebalancing" the split drifts as the assets grow at different rates.

The inflation-adjusted bond

The third asset isn't loaded from a file. It is built from your inflation data: each month it grows by that month's inflation plus one month's worth of the real yield you entered. It behaves like an inflation-protected bond held to maturity, with no price swings of its own. At a real yield of 0% it exactly keeps pace with inflation.

4. How the backtest works

A cycle is one complete retirement starting in a particular month of history. The backtest runs one cycle for every possible starting month: the first month of your data, then the second, and so on until a full retirement no longer fits. With 60 years of data and a 30-year retirement, that is about 360 cycles.

Within a cycle, each month goes through the same steps in the same order:

Step 1GrowEach asset's balance changes by that asset's own return for the month.
Step 2Inflate spendingThe monthly withdrawal rises by that month's inflation.
Step 3WithdrawSpending is taken from each asset in proportion to its share.
Step 4RebalanceIf this is a rebalancing month, reset to the target split.
The very first month is the exception: there is no growth or inflation yet, just the first withdrawal from the starting balance.

A cycle fails if its total value reaches $0 or below at any point before the end. The calculation keeps going after that, so a failed cycle shows a negative balance. Read that as how far short the plan fell, not as money you could actually have borrowed.

5. Reading the results

The summary

The text above the chart gives the number of cycles tested, the lowest, highest, and average ending balance, and the success rate: the share of cycles that never ran out.

The chart

The horizontal axis is years into retirement and the vertical axis is portfolio value. With hundreds of cycles, drawing every line would be an unreadable tangle, so the chart shows density instead:

  • Brightness shows how many cycles had that balance at that point in retirement. Bright bands are where most outcomes cluster; faint areas are rare outcomes.
  • Color shows how those cycles ended. Green means they survived, red means they eventually failed, and shades in between are a mix.
  • The dashed line is $0, the failure threshold.

Turn on Log scale to spread out the lower balances when a few very large outcomes squash everything else toward the bottom. A log axis has no place for zero or negative values, so failed cycles are flattened onto the bottom edge in that mode.

Exploring individual cycles

  • Hover over the chart to highlight the nearest cycle. The tooltip shows its start date, its balance at that point, and how far into retirement you are.
  • Hold Shift to lock onto the highlighted cycle. You can then move left and right along its whole history without the highlight jumping to a neighbor.
  • Click to pin a cycle. It stays drawn in blue and its month-by-month table opens below the chart.

The failed cycles list

Below the chart, every failed cycle is listed with its start date and the month it ran out. Click an entry to open that cycle's month-by-month table. Start dates that cluster together point to the stretch of history your plan is most exposed to. Click the panel's header to minimize or expand it.

7. What this can and can't tell you

  • History is one sample. A 100% success rate means the plan survived everything in your data, not everything that could happen.
  • Cycles overlap. A cycle starting in January and one starting in February share almost all of their months, so 360 cycles is far less than 360 independent tests. One bad era can account for a whole cluster of failures.
  • Recent start dates aren't tested. A 30-year cycle needs 30 years of data after its start, so the most recent decades only appear as the later years of older cycles.
  • Costs are left out. There are no taxes, fund fees, or trading costs, and spending never adjusts to how the portfolio is doing.
  • Your data sets the result. A price-only index, or data from a single country, changes the answer.

This is a tool for understanding how a plan behaves, not financial advice.
